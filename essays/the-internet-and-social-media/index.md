# The Emergence of Social Media 

When we think of social media in the modern era, we tend to think of using the internet to access websites and apps like Instagram, Facebook, TikTok, or Pintrest, just to name a few. Originally, the internet was not used by regular, every day people as it is now. Instead, it was used mainly by governments, military, and researchers as a way to communicate and transfer information securely. Social media was one of the first civillian-aimed parts of the internet that gave the average user access to people around the globe, new information, and most importantly, a new public sphere in a nonphysical realm. 

During the beginnings of social media in the early 1980s, social media looked a lot different. Forums, journals, blogs, and avatar-based games/programs were the face of social media in the early days of the internet. Some of the earliest, popular forums where people could interact on were the Computer Bulletin Board Systems, Communitree BBS, and the WELL. In these forums, people could engage in civil discourse, exchange ideas and perspectives, engage in spiritual and philosophical debates, and interact with other people across the globe in general. The aim of these forums typically revolved around creating a broader, global community of individuals using the internet casually to carve their online identities through their own written speech. However, using the internet to discuss technology and exchanging research, ideas, and more, were very common; sometimes more so than the casual forums.

Users looking for solely casual interaction with others in a fun, engaging manner would instead head over to these avatar-based programs, most notably LambdaMOO and MUD. In these spaces, users could cultivate their avatar in whatever way the want: how they looked, their gender, building digital homes, experimenting with personalities and even sexuality in some cases. These were places where the intimate aspects of life could be explored without shame, and the anonymity of these sites allowed people to feel safe, at the very least to some degree. 

Although the beginning of a new form of community offered new and exciting opportunities for many, there were also many critics who voiced their concerns and fears that the internet had to offer. Perhaps, an anonymous, unregulated, new landscape would not be as beneficial as hopeful internet users thought it could be.


## The Social Contract of 'Objective Truth'

Steven Shapin's *A Social History of Truth: Civility and Science in Seventeenth-Century England*, describes how the idea of 'objective truth' may not be as 'objective' as we may assume it to be. What is seen as a cold hard fact is instead influenced by a community's (whether the community in question be globally or locally) culture and feelings towards such claims, and who made it. The collection of knowledge and 'agreed upon' truths influence the moral and social bonds, and thereby the social contract, in which a society's foundation lies upon. The stability of a society rests on those, whether it be institutions or individuals, who are acknowledged and understood to be the symbols of 'truth'.  

In the time shortly before and immediately following the introduction of social media as a dominant and eventually inescapable form of communication and community building, the 'objective truth' of society came from institutions such as governments, news organizations, and health institutes. Individuals such as doctors, librarians, politicians, reporters, and teachers were also seen as members/extensions of these reliable institutions *(19)*. These people and organizations gained authority from a combination of expertise, institutional backing, and a historical/traditional understanding of respecting established systems of knowledge/intelligence. Authority had a tangible architecture; a claim could be traced back to where it came from and based on who said it, where, and how. Therefore, these claims could be accepted or rejected within a fairly stable framework. 

However, this does not necessarily entail that these institutions/social structure remained thoroughly neutral, nor did/could they ever attain perfection. Following from Shapin’s theory, the “objective truth” produced in this era was inflexible and catered to those with privilege, primarily the white, male majority opinion(s) of the world and their perception of reality. This disparity is also highlighted in what is coined as the 'Digital Divide' in both *Dr. Wilmon Brown*, and *Sin Chung Kai's* research, further discussed below.

Thus, as social media grew, people began to hope that the monolith would become more of a poly-lith. The 'objective truth' could come from people who had no chance at influencing this knowledge previously. Perhaps this traditional 'architecture' of authority could become a democracy, where a collectively maintained, continuously edited, and constantly growing pool of knowledge could indeed be an unbiased reflection of reality with the authority to societally make it so *(1)*. Social media had/has the capability to promote ‘groupmind’, which is a collective memory and intelligence shared across a community. 

Knowledge as a social contract could therefore be reevaluated through an inclusive, transparent, and adaptive lens via the internet’s ability to connect people globally, and social media’s ability to give the layman a platform to speak their truth *(4)*.

## The Immediate Responses/Reactions:

As social media began to gain traction and grow in popularity, so too did people's opinions on the matter. With the help of blogs, journals (such as LiveJournal), and eventually sites such as MySpace and Facebook, individuals could explain themselves not only to their families or friends, but also to the world what exactly their opinions were, and their thought processes. Reactions to the gaining popularity of social media varied. Some expected society to rally around this new technology to unify into something beautiful and unproblematic, whereas others feared the cultural, physical, and intellectual collapse of society as a consequence. 

Pierre Levy's *Collective Intelligence* describes the emerging social media outlets, including the earliest iterations of discussion boards, as a means to 'recreate the social bond' of human society, one that transcends the traditional understanding of intelligence, such as claims made by experts *(7)*. The dynamic environment of the internet allowed for the 'every-day' person to interact with others at neckbreaking speeds, creating a unique online culture and community bent on reimaging what and where knowledge could be obtained from *(7),(20)*. In essense, 'amateur' journalism became a core part of this, allowing others from across the world to get an intimate view of others' lives within their respective communities, rather than simply the broader pictures mainstream media outlets produced *(10)*. 

One of the most immediate and hopeful abilities of social media was that it created a way for humans to transmit and receive information faster than ever before. Where traditional media needed a whole cast of people to write, edit, produce, and publish it, social media allowed for anyone with internet access to disseminate whatever information they pleased, whenever they pleased, and however they wanted to. As the barrier to publication lowered, marginalized communities, especially those silenced due to censorship, propaganda, or general misrepresentation/oppression, were able to bypass the institutional gatekeeping of editors, producers, and publishers *(10)*. Social media could be a place where the members of a community could represent themselves and their experiences as they happened to an ever-growing, ever interested audience. The line between amateur journalism, where the news is perhaps unpolished, but deeply connected and accurate to the community it comes from, and professional journalism, where the news is smooth and cohesive, overarching and continuous, began to blur. The dissemination of information was no longer only delegated to traditional institutions, but rather anybody who wanted to.

This, of course, assumes that people will be truthful and altruistic, interested in cultivating communities and working with others towards a more peaceful world. To the optimists, especially in the beginning, this seemed likely to become reality. Social media could be a means of encouraging and facilitating the creation of world-wide communities *(4)*. There was hope that it would bring people together across cultures, languages, and continents, encouraging the collective accumulation of expertise and life experience which would then, of course, lead to a more peaceful world where education is no longer closed to those without the privilege to access it. A place where information is freely accessible, and where justice is equitably served. Social media would offer a safe space away from the harsh realities of the world, a new frontier just waiting to be democratized. 

For many, social media marked a new age not only for community building and access to information, but for political, social, and economic liberation. People living under authoritarian regimes, where speech was monitored and nonconformity punished, now had access to a place where, theoretically, they could say what they wanted and connect with people in similar situations *(1)*. Social media offered a place where people from all walks of life, from all types of philosophies, cultures, beliefs, and life experiences could be accepted wholly and entirely without prejudice. A new sovereign society could be born, one with its own rules, laws, and social contracts dictated by the altruistic whole; they would be democratic, inclusive, transparent, and adaptable. It would be a place of permanent self expression and revolution. A place where anybody, regardless of background, culture, or location, could participate equally.

Similarly, social media promised for some to be a place where the formation, exploration, and creation of identity could be done where and when one was comfortable. Especially after the introduction of avatar-based games such as LambdaMOO and proto-modern social media platforms such as MySpace, people, especially youth, turned to social media as a way to safely explore their identities *(14)*. The immediate social consequences from experimenting with sexuality, gender, religious beliefs, or politics in real, physical life were much easier to evade online. In particular, those who felt isolated, misunderstood, or unsafe expressing their true selves in their immediate, physical surroundings surroundings, found that social media promised an unstifling environment where one's identity was theirs and theirs alone.

However, where some saw hope in this frontier, others feared what may become of it. 

Because social media allowed people to bypass the editor, the producer, and the publisher, the information could not always be trusted. Unlike previous eras, a single piece of information is not so easily traced back to its source and so evaluating the trustworthiness of information that came from blogs would soon be understood to be incredibly difficult *(19)*. Anybody, from anywhere, with any motivation(s) can say anything online and somebody out there will fully believe them. As the means of getting real world information transitioned from something solely institutionalized to something that is a mix of established organizations, popular creators online, and from personal blogs, fact checking has become increasingly difficult. At its worst, social media cannot be trusted and can never be a means of transporting reliable information. This new frontier did not encourage telling the truth all the time, it encouraged interaction, which is a combination of reporting on shocking, eye-catching events and reporting all the time. Social media pushed journalists, amateur and professional, towards a lack of ‘fairness, accuracy, and truth’ and a rise in approaching reporting as a ‘publish, then filter’ kind of business (10). This lack of vetting makes it easy for misinformation to spread like wildfire.

In terms of community, critics argued that transitioning to online communities could cause a breakdown of real world connections, leading to isolation and a distrust towards fellow members of society. As the internet spread, it spread unevenly. Even now, there is a lack of access to the internet for many people around the world, a 'digital divide', which means that many people are left in the dark regarding this so called ‘democratization’ hoped for. This is the problem of the digital divide: access to the internet remains incredibly unequal, distributed along the fault lines of class, geography, age, and infrastructure *(2)*. In practice, the global village is only available to those who have access to it; a commodity. A privilege *(17)*. In addition, as online interaction began to be seen as an inevitable part of life, politics/politicians and corporations had an ever increasing want to grow their influence in the online world, viewing users as commodities who consume propaganda and capital rather than sovereign citizens. In the end, then, these virtual communities were neither as accessible nor as free as initially hoped for.

The safety of anonymity online extends, of course, to those with harmful intentions as well. Those who could not get away with widespread harm in the physical realm, could get away with it online, however.  One of the first instances of this exploitation of the anonymous identity was reported in Dibbell’s *A Rape in Cyberspace*, where a case of an online user (later found out to be a whole dorm floor telling the owner of the account what actions he should do) found a hack where he could force other users’ avatars to commit sexual acts against their wishes, on the online ‘game’ LambdaMOO *(6)*. This marked not only a wave of anger and frustration, but also one of the first instances where an online community banded together to enforce their newly-formed democratic space to vote and decide on the best form of action to take against the perpetrator: a ban. This coming-together of the online community showed that the democratic, harmonic, and altruistic vision many had was indeed a possibility, but the fear of the internet being a place where people could expose a darker side of themselves without the fear of the consequences of the law, began to tear through the fragile seams.

All of these converge upon fearing the quality of truth itself. Misinformation, which is false information spread without malicious intent, is already alarming enough, especially amplified in the world of social media. Disinformation, where false narratives are deliberately manufactured and spread to achieve social or political goals, is something entirely more dangerous *(5)*. If the internet was going to degrade the quality of truth, the ability for people to believe the truth, or for people to communicate effectively, this is the prime method of facilitating such harm. 

Social media platforms not only encourage engagement, often at the expense of presenting truthful information, but algorithmically sort people into neat categories, perfectly prepared to descend into echo chambers and hive minds: closed environments where claims, however dubious, are reinforced until they are simply 'fact' to those consuming it. The anonymity of the internet made it incredibly easy to impersonate expertise, to create an identity which seemed authoritative on a certain subject, and then use it maliciously. In Shapin's framework, truth is a perpetual social construction, dependent on trust, community, and shared belief in sources of authority *(15)*. Social media stands on this fragile thing and could cause a breakdown in how our society views truth and knowledge over time. Social media does not merely have the ability to spread false information, but rather it has the power to corrode the very infrastructure we've built to distinguish between fact and falsehood.

## But How is This Any Different From Past 'Disruptions'?

The rise of social media enabled anonymity and mass communication like no other. The rate at which mis/disinformation is created, spread, and believed is incredibly high and it is phenomenally hard to fix *(11)*. Disinformation campaigns have influenced elections, public perception, and have degraded our society’s trust in the truth, and what is accepted as the truth. It is so incredibly easy for an individual to be dishonest/spread disinformation and face practically no consequences for it. Anyone is able to say anything, from anywhere in the world, regardless of the 'truth value' of their content. The erasure of (formerly thought to be necessary) middlemen such as editors, publishers, and producers has lowered the bar for publications, thereby allowing anyone, regardless of their expertise on a subject, to act as an authority on a subject, or multiple for that matter. If anyone has the capabilities of doing this, that what even is 'truth' anymore? 

The rise of social media has led to the faster dissolution of trust in institutions and the collective judgements of truth or social bonds are no longer nearly as strong. In some ways, this is not a bad thing: people are able to express themselves and their views, but it also means that bad actors are abundant and prolific. The way we as a society think about truth and what it means to be ‘good’ information has forever changed with the advent of social media.

Both the internet and social media were once touted as tools for collective enlightenment, world peace, and shared knowledge. In practice, they have instead revealed that humans believe something not because it is backed up by evidence, but because it ‘feels right’. While we might like to think of ourselves as rational beings, we are in fact incredibly emotional, as evidenced by how we use the internet and in particular, social media *(3)*. 

Information is pushed more by algorithms than by users searching for answers, and those algorithms are based on engagement, and engagement is an incredibly emotional thing. This can lead to confirmation bias and the introduction or reinforcement of harmful ideologies. Disinformation and misinformation travel faster than actual information because 'untruths' often hit far harder emotionally *(12)*. Echo chambers are more prevalent than ever and this has caused people to isolate themselves within their own biases, creating hostile environments often completely based in emotion rather than logic. This has thus led to a rise in conspiracy theories and a distrust in scientifically supported data. This therefore has led to a breakdown in the social contract of trusting institutions and individuals historically and traditionally associated with 'truths'. 

The online sphere has become wrought with corporations, and the corporations that run the various social media platforms we use in the modern era, runs on engagement. Engagement is commodifie. It is easy to center social media in our daily lives: every moment of down time is spent online, every argument, every conversation, every thought, everything has to be paraded on the internet *(9)*. When this happens, there is a break down of social trust or bonding within family units. No longer can there be civil disagreements, or civil debates, even. The engagement factor, whether it be disinformation or misinformation pushed by these algorithms, is a foundation to this dismantling of trust, and harm to humans daily interactions, and bonds *(12)*. The more intense, emotion-provoking a topic, video, comment, or post is, the more it'll spread, and the harder it is to fight back against falsities. 

## Connection to AI

As AI becomes more and more ingrained in our lives, from chatbots soaring in popularity to search engines prioritizing AI results at the top of the page, there is an increased risk of developing ones own personal echo chamber. Similarly to social media, it is in this technology’s best interest to engage you. Social media has the capability to easily trap people into echo chambers, where their ideas, beliefs, and feelings are validated and reinforced constantly by other people of similar mindsets in similar situations. An AI echo chamber is something perhaps more insidious. It is one where a feedback loop happens not between a group of users, but a single user and a machine which has been trained to agree with them at every turn. When somebody turns repeatedly to AI rather than real people as an assistant, therapist, friend, or confidant, the diversity of perspective that is produced by real human reaction and interaction, is smoothed and amalgamated into something fluent, clean, assertive, and confident. This does not mean that this 'voice' is telling the truth, however, or is even acknowledging reality at all as it responds. AI often hallucinates and will confidently tell a user something which not only is false, but does not even have a sliver of a basis in reality. For users already inclined towards strong opinions or worldviews, an AI bot which mirrors, reproduces, and reinforces those opinions without pushback does not just deepen an echo chamber, it seals it into a fortified, defensive, personal bubble.

Compounding this risk is just how fast AI creates and disseminates information. Where human journalists, academics, and internet users require precious time to think, write, check, and publish information, an AI system can simply output a response, which upon superficial exampination, may read as coherent and fluent in mere seconds. Misinformation has always spread faster than any corrections could hope to, even before AI or bots at all *(5)*. Now, however, we are reaching an age where misinformation is not only spreading quickly, but created quickly, and created with a voice that demands attention and trust. It is a system created with content which does not require to be proofread by a human before it is let loose into the world. AI falsifies expertise and places itself as an authoritative figure, even though it's 'expertise' is simply data stripped from real, human experts. 

If you are not trained to catch the discrepancies, it is incredibly hard to refute in a large audience setting. AI does not just inherit the epistemic vulnerabilities social media gave birth to, it accelerates them to a speed which we are wholly unprepared to verify or even control at our current point in time. 

This is compounded by the false sense of personhood AI is good at replicating. In truth, a chatbot is simply an amalgamation of data with no experiences, no beliefs, no reason or ability to care about what 'truth' is, or what kind of information it is producing, or its quality. In practice, AI is seen as something person-like. Chatbots seem to be responsive, agreeable, understanding, and attentive. They 'act' as though they are capable of 'remembering' things, of having long lasting relationships, of being just another 'person' on the other side of the screen. However, this is not the case and it can be particularly dangerous to perceive an AI as human not only because of the echo chamber and (potential) misinformation issues mentioned above, but also because when someone turns to an AI ‘friend’ or ‘partner’, they are turning away from real human connection. This disconnect from reality emboldens a person to avoid learning conflict resolution skills, and social interaction as a whole *(18)*. Over time, this can be incredibly isolating and damaging for both individuals and communities.


## How I Used AI for This Essay

The sole AI used for the creation of this essay was Google's *NotebookLM*, and it was only used for technical help: research and notetaking/organizing purposes. At no point in time was AI used to generate titles, sentences, or to help refine my own, unique written work.  

With my chosen topic of the essay set in the early days of the internet, it was very difficult locating primary sources originating from the early 80's-2000's on my own. NotebookLM's AI was extremely useful in finding numerous sources from these periods. From previous experiences using AI, I knew I needed a sort of 'jumping off' point for the AI to find said sources, and so I started this process by uploading Steven Shapin's *A Social History of Truth: Civility and Science in Seventeenth-Century England*. From here I asked it to find sources that were relevant to Shapin's central themes of trust, (objective/subjective) truth, and the implications these have on humans' social construct/contract surrounding institutions that are meant to uphold these ideals. While there were most certainly sources that the AI presented to me that turned out to be irrelevant, most sources, however, *were* relevant and practical. Having Shapin's work as the backbone for my desired research and theme for this essay helped to maintain the AI's consistency throughout my time with it, thereby streamlining the research process for older sources, and  complimenting the newer, more modern sources I found in my time reseraching. 

Personally, I am someone who takes plenty of notes of the sources I read, from physical sticky notes to digital notes and highlights, but I end up leaving them scattered everywhere. This causes me to then allocate time trying to find and organize said notes, rather than working on the project in focus. The AI was able to keep all my notes visibile and accessible for me, giving me ease of mind knowing that I had a place to refer back and have help organizing my thoughts during the process. 

When I was ready to begin constructing an outline for my essay, NotebookLM's AI was able to take my notes and help me create a simplified outline of topics I felt were central to my chosen topic of discussion. I was able to use it as a sort of 'living curator', having the AI throw out points I felt were unnecessary, or even adding back in highlighted topics I previously felt cluttered up the article. 

There were moments, however, where the AI was not beneficial in my time reseraching and curating my outline. For example, in it's synopsis of the sources it generated, often times it would mistake the sources' citations as what the source itself was claiming to argue, when in reality the author may have been using said citations or quotes as a means of viewing the 'other side' of their argument. There were instances where it neglected to acknowledge sources I saved/found relevent in favor of consistently citing the same handful of sources, leading the initial outlines to be lackluster, and superficial to a degree.

From my time working with AI on this project, I conclude that the method in which I employed AI to help me formulate and organize my essay was extremely helpful, allowing me to focus on vetting information/sources, and writing the essay itself. While the vetting of sources took up a lot of time, time many would assume AI would help cut down, it instead helped me to refine and be on the constant look out of what I wanted my essay to cover, and include perspectives I felt were inherent to the discussion.
